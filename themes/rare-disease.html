Rare Disease

19 companies tracked

Rare diseases each affect fewer than 200,000 people in the U.S., but there are thousands of them, and most have no approved treatment. Many are caused by a single faulty gene, which makes them natural targets for gene therapy, RNA drugs and enzyme replacement. Small patient numbers are offset by orphan-drug incentives, faster regulatory paths and high prices.

The Science

Main approaches

  • Gene therapy uses a harmless virus (usually AAV) to deliver a working copy of a gene, often as a one-time treatment. Duchenne muscular dystrophy, hemophilia and Huntington's disease are active areas.
  • RNA drugs, including antisense oligonucleotides and siRNA, raise or lower production of a specific protein. Newer versions are linked to antibodies to reach muscle or brain.
  • Enzyme replacement and small molecules supply a missing enzyme, help a misfolded protein work, or block the harmful product of a disease pathway.

Why the economics differ

Orphan drug designation brings seven years of U.S. market exclusivity and tax credits. Priority review vouchers, which can be sold for around $100M or more, add to the incentives. Trials are small and often use accelerated approval based on a biomarker. That speeds approval, but confirmatory trials can fail and force a drug off the market. Gene therapies have also run into safety problems, including liver toxicity and deaths, which have led to label restrictions and clinical holds.

What investors watch

  • Biomarker vs functional results: whether the protein increase or biomarker change translates into better strength, survival or quality of life.
  • Launch metrics: patients identified, time to treatment and payer coverage. Rare-disease launches are slow and lumpy.
  • Safety across larger groups, especially for gene therapies given to older or sicker patients.
  • Manufacturing: viral vector supply and cost, which can cap the number of patients treated.
