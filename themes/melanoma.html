Melanoma

5 companies tracked

Checkpoint inhibitors turned advanced melanoma into a disease that many patients survive long-term. The biggest opportunity now is the roughly half of patients whose disease doesn't respond or relapses. Cell therapies, oncolytic viruses and T cell-redirecting drugs all target that group, and uveal (eye) melanoma is a separate, harder-to-treat disease.

The Science

Two different diseases

Cutaneous melanoma starts in pigment cells of the skin. Around half of cases carry a BRAF mutation, which can be treated with targeted BRAF/MEK inhibitor pills. Uveal melanoma starts in the eye, is driven by different mutations (GNAQ/GNA11), often spreads to the liver, and responds poorly to standard immunotherapy.

Standard treatment

Immune checkpoint inhibitors release the brakes on T cells. They include anti-PD-1 drugs (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab) and anti-LAG-3 (relatlimab, in Opdualag). Combinations produce durable responses in many patients, but a large share either don't respond or relapse. That "post-PD-1" setting is where most new drugs are tested first.

New approaches

  • TIL cell therapy (Amtagvi) expands the patient's own tumor-fighting T cells.
  • Oncolytic viruses (such as Tudriqev with nivolumab) are injected into tumors to kill cancer cells and stimulate an immune response.
  • T cell-redirecting drugs use targets inside cancer cells. KIMMTRAK (tebentafusp) became the first approved uveal melanoma drug in 2022, for patients with the HLA-A*02:01 tissue type. PRAME, a protein made by most melanomas, is targeted by both TCR cell therapies and ImmTAC drugs.
  • Targeted drugs for uveal melanoma, such as PKC inhibitors, act on the pathway that GNAQ/GNA11 mutations switch on.

What investors watch

  • Response rate and duration in single-arm trials, then confirmation in randomized trials measuring progression-free and overall survival.
  • HLA restriction: some therapies only work for patients with a specific tissue type, often about half of patients.
  • Ease of use: off-the-shelf drugs vs. therapies that need surgery, cell manufacturing or hospital stays.
  • Line of therapy: moving from later-line settings into first-line or post-surgery settings greatly expands the market.
